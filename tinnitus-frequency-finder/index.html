Tinnitus frequency finder

Most tinnitus has a pitch — a frequency, in hertz, that the ringing or hissing sits at. Finding yours takes about a minute: you listen to a tone, move it up and down, and stop when it sits at the same height as your own sound.

How pitch matching works

A pitch match is a comparison, not a measurement of your ear. You are the instrument: the app plays a pure tone and you decide when it matches. That is why it takes seconds rather than an audiology appointment, and also why it is an approximation rather than a clinical figure.

Most tinnitus pitches fall between 4,000 and 8,000 Hz, which is high — near the top of a piano and well above most speech. If your match lands far outside that range, it is worth doing it again in a quieter room.

Getting a reading you can trust

  • Use headphones, and a quiet room. Phone speakers roll off exactly the high frequencies you are trying to match.
  • Keep the volume low. You are matching pitch, not loudness. A quiet tone is easier to compare against.
  • Expect octave confusion. It is common to land an octave above or below — if 6,000 Hz feels close, try 3,000 and 12,000 and see which is really closer.
  • Match each ear separately if your tinnitus is different on each side.
  • Re-match occasionally. Tinnitus pitch can drift, and your first attempt is your least practised one.

What the number is actually for

On its own, a frequency is trivia. It becomes useful when the sound you listen to is built around it — which is what notched sound therapy does: it removes a narrow band around your pitch from the masking sound, so the sound sits around your tinnitus rather than on top of it.
